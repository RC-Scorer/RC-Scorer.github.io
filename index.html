Abstract

Ride comfort is largely settled at the moment a trajectory is chosen, yet autonomous driving planners score it with threshold tests on acceleration and jerk, with no frequency weighting and no reference to a vibration standard. Reaching ISO 2631-1 quantities instead requires survey-grade road input together with a per-vehicle identified dynamics model, neither of which production onboard perception provides.

This paper proposes RC-Scorer, which predicts ISO 2631-1 frequency-weighted body acceleration from onboard wheel-path road observations and ordinary driving logs, with no vehicle dynamics model and no parameter identification in the loop. The method has three components: a causal Transformer over the road geometry a candidate trajectory would traverse, an objective targeting the reported weighted RMS, and a frequency-response probe that tests what the network represents.

Using only a causal onboard reconstruction, RC-Scorer predicts av 2.2 times more accurately than a calibrated quarter-car given the survey-grade ground-truth surface, and reduces measured weighted vertical acceleration by up to 95 % in closed loop on a production vehicle.

Introduction

Reading road roughness out of a vehicle's own response is a mature inverse problem. The forward direction — predicting the response a road will produce — is sparse by comparison, and the reason is use rather than difficulty: it is only meaningful for a trajectory that has not been driven yet, and the place where untraveled trajectories must be evaluated is trajectory selection. Yet the de-facto standards for scoring an autonomous driving plan treat comfort as threshold tests on acceleration, jerk and yaw rate, applying no frequency weighting and referring to no whole-body vibration standard.

Comfort in physical units has been reached along two routes, each paying a different price. The physical route puts a vehicle model in the loop and assumes a survey-grade profile; that premise is structural, because forward integration takes the second spatial derivative of the profile and so amplifies whatever noise the road estimate carries. The learning route escapes that premise but gives up the output, emitting dimensionless scalars, variances or section-aggregate statistics without axis weighting. It also gives up any means of checking what was learned, since output error alone cannot separate a model that acquired vehicle dynamics from one that memorised dataset statistics.

RC-Scorer reaches ISO 2631-1 axis-weighted body acceleration from the degraded road estimate a production perception stack emits, using ordinary driving logs as the only supervision. The score is re-evaluated per untraveled candidate and consumed onboard as a planning cost.

Contributions

  • 1 Vehicle-model-free comfort prediction. ISO 2631-1 weighted body acceleration is obtained from a causal onboard road reconstruction without a dynamics model or parameter identification, outperforming calibrated physical baselines that are given the survey-grade ground-truth surface.
  • 2 Frequency-response validation. The learned road-to-body response is compared against measurement and a fitted quarter-car, showing that the network reproduces the body mode and its speed dependence, and identifying the band where it does not.
  • 3 Candidate-conditional closed-loop scoring. The prediction is re-evaluated per untraveled candidate trajectory and used onboard a production vehicle, reducing measured weighted vertical acceleration over obstacles while holding the lane center where avoidance is the wrong answer.

Method

Let τk be the candidate trajectories a planner emits at one cycle. For each candidate the road height under the four tire contact points is extracted along that candidate, together with its curvature and speed profile and the current vehicle state. The predictor maps these to the three-axis body acceleration waveform over the next T seconds, from which ISO 2631-1 weighting yields a scalar av(τk) that the planner consumes as a comfort cost. No vehicle dynamics model and no identified parameters appear in this chain.

Onboard road observation as input

Because forward integration differentiates the profile twice, the physical route needs a road estimate whose noise survives that amplification, and a production perception stack does not produce one. The road channel is therefore taken as perception emits it, retaining dropout, measurement noise and the range- and incidence-dependent precision loss of the sensor. Unobserved stretches are filled by linear interpolation, whose error against the observed ground truth is 1.8 mm for gaps of 14 samples or fewer — the smallest of the alternatives in the 4–8 Hz band where Wk is most sensitive. Cubic splines diverge on 49.5 % of gaps longer than 15 samples.

Causal Transformer predictor

Future road geometry cannot cause present vibration, and the architecture enforces this with a causal mask over self-attention rather than relying on the data to teach it. Inputs are the four wheel-path road channels, the candidate trajectory, and an eleven-channel vehicle state holding physical quantities at t0 only. The model carries no explicit vehicle representation: no latent vehicle embedding, no conditioning modulation and no identified parameters — a choice the ablation argues for rather than merely permits.

Training objective for the weighted RMS

A time-domain squared error alone shrinks predicted amplitude systematically, and the effect is structural rather than an optimisation failure: at the least-squares solution the regression slope of measurement on prediction converges to the squared correlation. Because the reported quantity is a weighted RMS, that quantity is placed in the objective — a waveform term, a relative band-energy term, and a moment term on the av distribution. The log ratio makes the band penalty relative, so quiet bands are not swamped by loud ones.

ISO 2631-1 reduction

Each predicted axis waveform is filtered by the weighting ISO 2631-1 prescribes for that direction — Wd horizontal, Wk vertical — and the three axes combine into the vibration total value av. Where a signal is dominated by shocks the basic evaluation understates exposure, so MTVV, VDV and the crest factor are reported alongside. On the measured signals 0.34 % of windows exceed a crest factor of 9 and 14.66 % exceed VDV/(awT1/4) = 1.75, which is why VDV is reported with the RMS. The evaluation is body-referenced rather than seat-referenced.

Frequency-response validation probe

Output error alone cannot distinguish a model that represents vehicle dynamics from one that memorised dataset statistics. The road-to-vertical-acceleration frequency response is therefore estimated and three curves compared: measured road to measured IMU, measured road to predicted response, and the fitted quarter-car. The H1 estimator with Welch ensembling is used and frequencies where ordinary coherence falls below 0.3 are gated; the gate is fixed in advance. Windows are stratified into three speed bins, because wheelbase filtering makes the apparent body-mode peak move with speed — a model that has learned the vehicle should reproduce that movement, and a fixed-parameter model cannot.

Candidate-conditional re-evaluation

At each planning cycle the road grid is re-sampled under each candidate's own wheel paths and the predictor is re-run, so a candidate that has never been driven still receives a score derived from the geometry it would actually traverse. The road encoder output is shared across candidates, so the marginal cost per additional candidate is small. The planner consumes av(τk) as an additive comfort cost, protected by a selection hysteresis margin, a lateral deadband and a per-cycle rate limit.

Obstacle layouts

Six layouts were built for the closed-loop test, each putting a different question to the scorer. S1–S3 place speed bumps under one or both wheel tracks. S4 places bumps of unequal height at the same longitudinal station, so no candidate avoids both and the correct behaviour is to move toward the lower one. S5 is a pothole field. S6 places the obstacles between the wheel tracks, where lateral motion is the wrong answer.

Experiments

Setup

Offline evaluation uses a multi-country road dataset that provides four-wheel contact-point road height, the planned trajectory and synchronised IMU simultaneously, on its official validation split of 29 sequences and 13,875 windows. Two road conditions are evaluated throughout: Road A, the offline contact-point ground truth, and Road B, reconstructed online from only the LiDAR returns acquired up to t0 — the observation a production stack actually has at decision time.

Closed-loop evaluation runs on a production battery-electric SUV. A roof-mounted 128-beam lidar at 10 Hz is accumulated into a ground-anchored grid, from which four wheel-path height sequences (4 × 500 samples at 100 Hz over 5 s) are extracted per candidate; ground truth is a 100 Hz navigation-grade inertial unit. The lattice planner emits five candidates at lateral offsets 0, ±1.25 and ±2.5 m, with a hysteresis margin of 0.15, a 0.3 m deadband and a 0.26 m per-cycle rate limit. Cost age at the planner is 49 ms median and 56 ms at the 90th percentile against a 0.4 s gate.

Test site

Every paired run was driven on the same course at a proving ground. Two sections were used: a straight multi-lane section for the bump layouts, and a narrow walled corridor for the pothole field.

Offline accuracy against physical baselines

The predictor is compared against three physical routes on identical wheel-path inputs, identical speed profiles and one shared ISO weighting implementation, so differences are attributable to the response model alone: an IRI regression on the ASTM E1926 golden car, the Ahlin–Granlund spectral method, and a golden-car quarter-car integrated forward.

The first result is a capability gap rather than an accuracy gap: the physical baselines cannot generate the horizontal axes at all. On the vertical axis, from Road A to Road B the av MAE of the proposed model rises by 1.50× (0.0424 → 0.0635 m s−2), against 3.34× for the quarter-car and 3.49× for Ahlin–Granlund. Using only the degraded onboard observation, the predictor is 2.22× more accurate than a calibrated quarter-car given the survey-grade ground-truth surface.

The collapse of the quarter-car and Ahlin–Granlund on Road B is an amplification rather than a uniform loss: their aw,z RMSE reaches 0.9203 and 1.1042, above the 0.2888 of predicting identically zero. An open-loop forward integration with no noise-rejection term turns the high-frequency content of a causal LiDAR surface into wheel-hop energy the vehicle never experienced.

Ablation and frequency response

Six ways of giving the network an explicit vehicle representation were tested — latent embeddings of dimension 4 and 64, FiLM modulation, conditional LayerNorm, and per-token SPADE modulation. All six degraded held-out accuracy while training loss decreased monotonically with capacity. The learned embedding explains the gap: the vector identifies which training run a window came from, and a held-out run is by construction one the model has never identified. The predictor has already absorbed the vehicle's characteristics from ordinary logs, so supplying them explicitly adds a degree of freedom that can only be spent on memorisation.

In the frequency domain the measured body-mode peak moves across the three speed bins and the predictor reproduces that movement, while the fitted quarter-car places it at 1.60 Hz in every bin, as it must. The claim is bounded accordingly: the predictor represents the body mode and its speed dependence, and not the wheel-hop band, where near 12 Hz the prediction is low and the quarter-car is better.

Closed-loop results

A paired design: for each layout the vehicle drove the same course twice, scorer disabled and enabled, at two speed settings (S5 at the lower setting only), giving eleven pairs. In all eleven the struck interval and the passed interval occupy the same longitudinal range, so the pairs compare the same geometry. The judged quantity is aw,z rather than av, because the vibration total value mixes in the manoeuvre that avoidance itself produces.

Where full avoidance is available the in-zone weighted vertical acceleration falls by 87 % to 95 % and the peak by 92 % to 97 %. S4 behaves as designed rather than as a failure: with no clean candidate the planner moves toward the lower obstacle, and the residual 53–61 % reduction is the difference between striking a low bump and a high one.

S6 carries the most weight. With the obstacles between the wheel tracks, any lateral motion would place a wheel on them and the correct behaviour is to do nothing. The scorer selected the center in 92 % and 91 % of cycles and the planner applied it in 99 %, with no departure at either speed. The mechanism is visible in the per-candidate predictions: over the approach the five candidates' predicted aw,z spread from 0.15 to 0.40 m s−2, with the center at the bottom of that spread. The model is not preferring lateral motion and being restrained; it is scoring the center as the best option.

Checking the prediction against measurement in the same recordings, the avoidance windows agree within a factor of 0.75–2.1 and the center-holding windows within 0.82–0.95. Where a wheel actually strikes an obstacle the prediction is low by a factor of two to four — the amplitude compression documented offline reappearing at the high end — although candidate ordering, which is what selection depends on, remains correct there.

Limitations

The closed-loop campaign is small and partly uncontrolled: each cell is a single run, so the reductions are point estimates, and in the higher-speed cells the enabled run traversed the obstacle more slowly than the disabled run, so those rows conflate avoidance with slowing and the claim rests on the speed-matched pairs. The cost of avoiding is not quantified. Amplitude compresses where it matters most. The horizontal axes hold offline but not in deployment, which is why aw,z and not av is the closed-loop criterion. Two operating points are shown rather than a degradation curve, and the wheel-hop band is not represented. Deployment is shown, necessity is not: the closed-loop results establish that a physical-unit comfort prediction changes the chosen trajectory, not that ISO frequency weighting is required for that change.
